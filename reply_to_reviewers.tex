\reviewer{Reviewer 1}
\begin{reviewcomment}
The adversary is assumed to reconstruct the transmit signals of individual
APs from its received observations. Since the received signal contains a
superposition of multiple AP transmissions, it is unclear how these
components can be reliably separated, especially in the presence of AN and
imperfect CSI.
\end{reviewcomment}
\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this important comment and agree that the
separation step requires an explicit treatment. Since all APs transmit common
symbols, the aggregate observation
$\mathbf{y}_a[n]=\sum_{k}\mathbf{H}_{a,k}\mathbf{x}_k[n]+\mathbf{n}_a[n]$
provides only $M_{\mathrm{UE}}$ observation dimensions per snapshot for
$N_{\mathrm{tx}}M_{\mathrm{tx}}$ per-AP transmit dimensions. Blind separation
of the per-AP components from $\mathbf{y}_a[n]$ alone is therefore not
possible in general, and we do not claim otherwise. The original manuscript
did not state clearly that this step is an assumption, and we have revised
the threat model accordingly.

Following the threat model of [12], the revised manuscript explicitly adopts
a \emph{genie-aided strong-adversary model}, in which the per-AP components
of the received samples are made available to the adversary. We adopt this
model deliberately, for the following reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Conservative privacy assessment.} The genie removes the
  inter-AP superposition, which is the main obstacle for a practical
  adversary seeking AP-dependent directions. The privacy of the proposed
  design is therefore assessed against an adversary that is granted a
  capability that a practical adversary does not have in general.
  \item \emph{Plausibility.} Partial per-AP separation may become available
  in practice, e.g., when AP-specific transmissions are resolvable in time
  or frequency, or when the propagation delays of the distributed APs are
  resolvable over a wide bandwidth.
  \item \emph{Design principle.} The network cannot verify the receiver
  capability of a served user. A privacy mechanism should therefore not
  rely on the adversary being unable to separate the per-AP signals.
\end{enumerate}

Regarding the AN, it is intentionally contained in the per-AP components
available to the adversary. Keeping the AN visible in the raw observations,
while nulling it at the legitimate combiner outputs, is the
receive-processing asymmetry on which the proposed design relies. Regarding
imperfect CSI, the adversary's channel-estimation error enters the residual
term $\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{a,k}^{\mathrm{adv}}[n]$ of the signal model.
Consistent with the strong-adversary model, the simulated adversary is
further given accurate knowledge of its per-AP channels $\mathbf{H}_{a,k}$,
which is now stated in Section~IV. The identifiability of the per-AP
reconstruction step is discussed in our response to Reviewer~2, Comment~2.
\end{response}

\begin{reviewcomment}
Constraint (9g) does not necessarily guarantee that the fake direction
becomes the dominant peak of the reconstructed beampattern. Moreover, the
condition used to derive (10) is not explicitly enforced in the
optimization.
\end{reviewcomment}
\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this careful observation and agree with both
points. Under the ideal covariance decomposition, the reconstructed
true-direction response is $\widehat{B}_k(\phi_k)=S_k+T_k$, where
$S_k=\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_{-a,k}\|_F^2$ and
$T_k=|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{z}_k|^2$. The original constraint (9g)
bounds $S_k$ through the fake-direction response
$F_k=|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k})\mathbf{z}_k|^2$, but places no
restriction on $T_k$, i.e., on the AN response in the true direction. The
fake-direction response could therefore fall below the true-direction
response even when (9g) holds, and the condition $T_k/F_k\le1/M_{\mathrm{tx}}$
used to derive (10) was indeed not enforced. We have revised the design as
follows.

\textbf{(i) Revised fake-peak constraint.} We now include the
true-direction AN response in the constraint itself:
\begin{equation*}
\left|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k})\mathbf{z}_k\right|^2
\ge\rho\left\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\right\|_F^2
+\left|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{z}_k\right|^2,\quad k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}.
\tag{9g$'$}
\end{equation*}
Since the remaining terms of $\widehat{B}_k(\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k})$ are
nonnegative, $\widehat{B}_k(\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k})\ge F_k$, and since
$S_k\le\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\|_F^2$, (9g$'$) directly gives
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{B}_k(\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k})-\widehat{B}_k(\phi_k)
\ge F_k-S_k-T_k
\ge(\rho-1)\left\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\right\|_F^2.
\tag{10$'$}
\end{equation*}
Hence, $\rho>1$ is sufficient for the fake-direction response to exceed the
true-direction response, and this guarantee now follows from a constraint
that the optimization enforces, without any auxiliary condition. The
previous sufficient condition $\rho>M_{\mathrm{tx}}/(M_{\mathrm{tx}}-1)$ is
replaced by $\rho>1$.

\textbf{(ii) Tractability.} In the CCCP update, (9g$'$) becomes
$\rho\,\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{W}_k^H\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{W}_k)
+\mathbf{z}_k^H\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{z}_k-\widetilde{\zeta}_k\le0$, where
$\widetilde{\zeta}_k$ is the affine lower bound of $F_k$. This is a convex
quadratic constraint. Since $\widetilde{\zeta}_k\le F_k$ globally, every
point feasible for the surrogate is feasible for (9g$'$), so the
feasibility, monotonicity, and convergence arguments of Section~III-B carry
over unchanged.

\textbf{(iii) Resolvability.} The comparison in (10$'$) is meaningful only
when the $M_{\mathrm{tx}}$-element array can resolve the true and fake
directions. We now make this explicit by imposing (9g$'$) on
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}=\{k:|\sin\phi_k-\sin\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k}|\ge2/M_{\mathrm{tx}}\}$,
i.e., on the APs whose two directions are at least one Rayleigh width
apart. With the original virtual location
$\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{f}}=(5,-25)$~m, the target and the virtual location
were nearly collinear with three transmit APs, for which
$|\sin\phi_k-\sin\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k}|\approx0.03$--$0.04<2/M_{\mathrm{tx}}=0.125$,
so no distinguishable fake peak could be formed at these APs. We have
therefore relocated the virtual location to
$\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{f}}=(175,35)$~m, for which all eight transmit APs
satisfy the condition, with
$\min_k|\sin\phi_k-\sin\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k}|=0.163$, so that
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}$ contains all transmit APs. As explained in our
response to Reviewer~2, Comment~5, this full support is also what makes the
true and fake hypotheses symmetric for a multi-hypothesis adversary. The set
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}$ is kept in the formulation, since it determines
where the constraint is meaningful in a general geometry.

\textbf{(iv) From the true direction to the dominant peak.} We agree that
(10$'$) compares the fake direction with the true direction only, and that
it holds for the ideal covariance. It does not guarantee that
$\phi_{\mathrm{fake},k}$ is the global maximum over all $\theta$, since the
beams of the other users produce responses in their own directions, and the
finite number of snapshots and the EM reconstruction error add residual
cross terms. We have therefore revised the manuscript to claim only what is
guaranteed, namely that the fake-direction response exceeds the
true-direction response, and we evaluate the location of the reconstructed
dominant peak numerically. For localization deception, the relevant event is
that the dominant peak is displaced from the true direction, since a peak
elsewhere also removes that AP's direction line from the target cell. At
$\eta\approx0.50$, over the APs in $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}$, the
reconstructed fake-direction response exceeds the true-direction response in
$[X]\%$ of the cases, and the dominant peak lies at the fake direction, the
true direction, and elsewhere in $[X]\%$, $[X]\%$, and $[X]\%$ of the cases,
respectively.

\textbf{(v) Updated results.} All results have been recomputed with
(9g$'$). At $\eta\approx[X]$, the proposed design now reduces the
localization success rate to $[X]\%$, with a sensing SNR of $[X]$~dB.
\end{response}

\begin{reviewcomment}
The sensing SNR expression in (2) needs further clarification. The
contributions from different APs are added without considering possible
coherent terms. In addition, the AN waveform is assumed to be known at the
legitimate sensing receivers, but its contribution is excluded from the
sensing SNR.
\end{reviewcomment}
\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this comment. We have revised the sensing model in
Section~II to state the receiver processing and the assumptions behind (2)
explicitly, and we address the two points below.

\textbf{Coherent cross terms.} Since all transmit APs radiate common symbols,
the echo received at receive AP $r$ is the superposition
$\sum_k\alpha_{r,k}\sqrt{g_{r,k}}\,\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{s}[n]$,
where $\mathbf{s}[n]$ collects the symbols and $\alpha_{r,k}$ is the complex
bistatic echo coefficient with $\mathbb{E}\{|\alpha_{r,k}|^2\}=\xi_{r,k}^2$.
Its instantaneous power therefore contains cross terms between transmit APs,
whose values depend on the relative phases of $\{\alpha_{r,k}\}_k$. These
phases are not known to the network, because the target position is known
only coarsely relative to the carrier wavelength ($\lambda_c\approx15.8$~cm
at $1.9$~GHz) and the target response varies with the aspect angle. We
therefore model them as independent and uniformly distributed across
transmit--receive AP pairs. Under this model, the cross terms vanish in
expectation, and (2) is the per-snapshot sensing SNR averaged over the echo
phases, which is why it is referred to as the \emph{average} sensing SNR.
The revised manuscript now states this assumption explicitly. A design that
exploits coherent superposition at the target would require phase-accurate
knowledge of the target position and phase synchronization of the transmit
APs at the target, which we do not assume for the sensing function.

\textbf{AN contribution.} The reviewer is correct that the AN echo was
excluded from (2) without explanation. Since $s_z[n]$ is known at the receive
APs and independent of the data and sensing symbols, its echo is separated by
matched filtering and could in principle be exploited. We keep it out of the
design objective deliberately. The AN echo from the true target is
proportional to $T_k=|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{z}_k|^2$, so including it
in the objective would reward AN power in the true direction. This would
raise the true-direction peak of the beampattern reconstructed by the
adversary and work against the fake-peak constraint. Exploiting the AN echo
at the receivers after the design would add $\sum_kc_kT_k\ge0$ to
$\gamma_t$. Hence, the reported $\gamma_t$ is a lower bound on the achievable
sensing SNR, and the reported $3.6$~dB loss is an upper bound on the sensing
cost of the proposed design. Both points are now stated in Sections~II
and~III.
\end{response}

\begin{reviewcomment}
There is a potential inconsistency in the sensing-cost model. Equation (2)
defines the sensing SNR using only the communication/sensing beam matrix
$\mathbf {W_k} $ and excludes the AN beam $\mathbf {z_k} $, while the
manuscript later states that $s_z[n]$ is known at the sensing receivers and
that its echo can be separated from $s_t[n]$ by ideal matched filtering. If
$s_z[n]$ is known, its target echo is itself a known probing signal and can,
in principle, contribute additional sensing information; if it is
deliberately discarded, the reported $3.6$ dB sensing-SNR loss is specific to
that receiver definition. Can the sensing signal model explicitly state
whether the AN echo is exploited, discarded, or treated as interference?
\end{reviewcomment}
\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for pointing out this inconsistency. The reviewer is
correct that the known AN echo is itself a probing signal, and that the
original manuscript did not state how it is handled. The revised sensing
model now states explicitly that the AN echo is \emph{separated and
discarded}: since $s_z[n]$ is known at the receive APs and independent of the
data and sensing symbols, matched filtering separates its echo, and it is
neither exploited in (2) nor treated as interference. The residual
cross-correlation between independent unit-power symbols after matched
filtering decays as $1/N$ in power and is neglected for $N=128$.

We discard the AN echo in the design deliberately, rather than for
convenience. The AN echo from the true target is proportional to
$T_k=|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{z}_k|^2$. Including it in the objective
would therefore reward AN power in the true direction, which raises the
true-direction peak of the beampattern reconstructed by the adversary and
works against the fake-peak constraint (9g).

We also agree that the $3.6$~dB loss is specific to this receiver
definition, and we have qualified it accordingly. If the receivers
additionally exploit the AN echo, the sensing SNR becomes
$\gamma_t+\sum_kc_kT_k\ge\gamma_t$. The reported $\gamma_t$ is therefore a
lower bound on the achievable sensing SNR, and the reported $3.6$~dB loss is
an upper bound on the sensing cost of the proposed design. In addition, our
response to Reviewer~3, Comment~2, translates this loss into detection and
false-alarm probabilities.
\end{response}

\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and fully agree with the
dimension count. Since the APs transmit common symbols, $\mathbf{y}_a[n]$
offers $M_{\mathrm{UE}}=16$ observation dimensions per snapshot, against
$N_{\mathrm{tx}}M_{\mathrm{tx}}=128$ per-AP transmit dimensions in the
default setting. The decomposition of $\mathbf{y}_a[n]$ into per-AP
components is therefore underdetermined, and the original manuscript did not
state clearly that this step is an assumption rather than a consequence of
the signal model. We have revised the threat model to separate the attack
into two stages and to state the identifiability status of each.

\textbf{(i) Per-AP separation (assumed).} The separation of
$\mathbf{y}_a[n]$ into per-AP components is not identifiable from
$\mathbf{y}_a[n]$ alone in general. Following [12], we now state explicitly
that we adopt a genie-aided strong-adversary model, in which the per-AP
observations $\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{a,k}[n]$ are available to the

\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and agree with its
conclusion. The reported reduction certifies robustness against the
dominant-peak reconstruction rule of [12], and we have revised the claims of
the manuscript accordingly. Below, we identify the property that governs a
multi-hypothesis adversary, state what the proposed design can and cannot
guarantee against it, and report its performance against such an adversary.

\textbf{What the AN can and cannot do.} The AN symbol $s_z[n]$ is
independent of the sensing symbol $s_t[n]$, so the AN adds terms to the
reconstructed covariance but cannot cancel the true-direction component
$\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_{-a,k}$, which the legitimate sensing
requires. Against an adversary that retains several peaks, the AN
therefore creates a competing hypothesis rather than erasing the true one.
Privacy against such an adversary then means that the adversary cannot tell
the true hypothesis from the artificial one.

\textbf{The governing property: statistical symmetry of the hypotheses.}
The adversary knows neither $s_t[n]$ nor $s_z[n]$. Both are zero-mean,
unit-variance, mutually independent, and independent of the adversary's own
data, so the reconstructed covariance contains the true-direction and
fake-direction components as rank-one terms driven by unknown streams with
identical statistics. By construction, the fake directions of the APs in
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}$ are geometrically consistent with the common
virtual location $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{f}}$, just as the true directions are
consistent with $\mathbf{p}_t$. This symmetry, however, also requires the
fake hypothesis to be supported by the same APs as the true one. An AP whose
true and fake directions are unresolvable supports only the true
hypothesis, so that a vote-counting adversary favors the target unless
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{res}}$ contains all transmit APs. In the revised
simulations, we therefore choose $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{f}}=(175,35)$~m, for which
all eight transmit APs satisfy the resolvability condition of our response
to Reviewer~1, Comment~2. With full support, the two hypotheses differ only
in their peak powers, which are set by $\rho$ in (9g). If the two powers are
matched, the peak powers carry no information on which hypothesis is
artificial, and an adversary that relies on the reconstructed beampatterns
faces two hypotheses that are, up to sidelobe details, symmetric. It can
then identify the target with probability at most one half of the
probability that the target is among the hypotheses, and with $L$ virtual
locations this bound becomes $1/(L+1)$. We note that the symmetry is not
complete: the true-direction response collects several unknown streams,
whereas the fake-direction response is generated by the single AN stream,
so an adversary that analyzes the eigen-structure of
$\mathbf{R}_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_k}$ could in principle exploit this
difference. Spreading the AN over several streams to remove it is noted as
an extension. Conversely, with $\rho>1$, the fake
peak is systematically the stronger one: this maximizes the deception of the
dominant-peak attack, but an adversary that knows the mechanism can exploit
the power asymmetry.

\textbf{Design implication.} The parameter $\rho$ therefore trades the
deception of a dominant-peak adversary against the indistinguishability of
the hypotheses for a mechanism-aware adversary. To make the matched-power
operating point available, the design now optionally caps the fake-direction
response, $F_k\le\kappa(\rho\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\|_F^2+T_k)$
with $\kappa\ge1$, which in the CCCP update is again a convex constraint
when its right-hand side is replaced by its affine lower bound. With
$\rho\approx1$, the two peak powers are then matched to within the factor
$\kappa$. In addition, $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{f}}$ should be defined relative
to the target position, e.g., by a fixed offset, so that an adversary
observing several frames cannot single out the artificial hypothesis as the
one that does not follow the target's motion.

\textbf{Evaluation.} The revised manuscript evaluates a multi-hypothesis
adversary. It retains the two strongest distinct peaks of each
$\widehat{B}_k(\theta)$, casts one direction line per peak, forms the two
most-voted, geometrically separated location hypotheses, and resolves them
by the vote count, at random, or, knowing that the fake peak is designed to
be the stronger one, by choosing the hypothesis supported by the weaker
peaks. The success of an adversary allowed two guesses bounds every rule

\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this comment and agree that the legitimate sensing
operation was not described in sufficient detail. The revised Section~II now
describes the processing chain as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Operation at each RX AP.} Each receive AP employs
  $M_{\mathrm{rx}}$ antennas and steers its receive beam toward the target.
  The resulting receive array gain is absorbed, together with the target
  RCS, in the scattering power $\xi_{r,k}^2$. Each receive AP then
  matched-filters its received samples with the known transmitted symbols.
  The data symbols are available at the central processor and therefore act
  as additional probing signals, while the echo of the known AN symbol is
  separated by matched filtering and discarded.
  \item \emph{Combining between RX APs.} The receive APs forward their
  matched-filter outputs to the central processor, which fuses them
  non-coherently. Non-coherent fusion avoids phase synchronization among the
  receive APs.
  \item \emph{How (2) is obtained.} At receive AP $r$, the echo is the
  superposition
  \begin{equation*}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tx}}}\alpha_{r,k}\sqrt{g_{r,k}}\,\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{s}[n],
  \end{equation*}
  where $\mathbf{s}[n]$ collects the unit-power symbols and $\alpha_{r,k}$ is
  the bistatic echo coefficient with $\mathbb{E}\{|\alpha_{r,k}|^2\}=\xi_{r,k}^2$.
  Since the echo phases are not known to within the carrier wavelength, they
  are modeled as independent and uniformly distributed across
  transmit--receive AP pairs. Averaging over these phases and the symbols
  gives the per-snapshot SNR at receive AP $r$,
  \begin{equation*}
  \gamma_r=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{rx}}^2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tx}}}\xi_{r,k}^2g_{r,k}\left\|\mathbf{a}^H(\phi_k)\mathbf{W}_k\right\|_F^2,
  \end{equation*}
  and (2) is its average $\gamma_t=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{rx}}}\sum_r\gamma_r$.
  \item \emph{Who experiences the SNR.} $\gamma_t$ is the average
  per-receive-AP SNR. The SNR of the fused statistic at the central
  processor is $N_{\mathrm{rx}}\gamma_t$. Since the two differ by a constant
  factor, they are maximized by the same beams, and the design is unaffected
  by this choice. All reported sensing SNRs are average per-receive-AP
  values, which is now stated in Section~IV.
\end{enumerate}
The detector applied to the fused statistic is described in our response to

\begin{reviewcomment}
Furthermore, the authors have not described any detector or detection
threshold. Therefore, ``x dB sensing SINR loss'' doesn't represent anything.
How is it translated into the detection and false-alarm probabilities,
which eventually matter? Without this, it is very difficult to judge whether

\begin{response}
We thank the reviewer for this important comment and agree that the SNR loss
should be related to detection performance. The revised manuscript now
specifies the detector and reports the corresponding detection
probabilities.

\textbf{Detector.} Each receive AP matched-filters its samples with the
$N_{\mathrm{s}}=N_{\mathrm{UE}}+1$ known data and sensing streams. With
unknown echo amplitudes and phases, the generalized likelihood ratio test
sums the matched-filter output energies over the $N_{\mathrm{s}}$ streams and
the $N_{\mathrm{rx}}$ receive APs, and compares the sum with a threshold
$\tau$. After normalization, the statistic follows a central $\chi^2$
distribution with $2N_{\mathrm{rx}}N_{\mathrm{s}}$ degrees of freedom under
the null hypothesis, so $\tau$ is set by
$\Pr\{\chi^2_{2N_{\mathrm{rx}}N_{\mathrm{s}}}>\tau\}=P_{\mathrm{fa}}$. Under
the target hypothesis, it follows a noncentral $\chi^2$ distribution with
noncentrality $2N\sum_r\gamma_r$, which gives
\begin{equation*}
P_{\mathrm{d}}=\mathbb{E}\Big\{Q_{N_{\mathrm{rx}}N_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big(\sqrt{2N{\textstyle\sum_r}\gamma_r},\sqrt{\tau}\Big)\Big\},
\end{equation*}
where $Q_{M}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the generalized Marcum $Q$-function of order
$M$. Consistent with the random echo phases of the sensing model, the
per-receive-AP SNRs $\gamma_r$ are taken as independent and exponentially
distributed with mean $\gamma_t$, and the expectation is over this
fluctuation. Among fluctuation models with the same mean, this is a
conservative choice in the high-$P_{\mathrm{d}}$ regime.

\textbf{Detection performance.} For $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=10^{-6}$,
$N_{\mathrm{rx}}=4$, and $N_{\mathrm{s}}=5$, $P_{\mathrm{d}}=0.9$ requires
$\gamma_t\ge-8.5$~dB with integration over the $N=128$ snapshots. The
proposed design at $\eta\approx0.50$ ($\gamma_t=11.1$~dB) therefore retains
a margin of $19.6$~dB, compared with $23.2$~dB for the no-AN reference
($\gamma_t=14.7$~dB), and both designs achieve $P_{\mathrm{d}}>0.999$. Even
with the shortest integration that resolves all streams, $N=N_{\mathrm{s}}=5$,
the proposed design achieves $P_{\mathrm{d}}=0.998$, compared with $0.9999$
for the no-AN reference. The $3.6$~dB loss therefore reduces the detection
margin rather than the detection probability in the default setting.

\textbf{Practical interpretation.} Since the two-way path gain $g_{r,k}$
scales with the fourth power of distance, a $3.6$~dB loss corresponds to an
approximately $19\%$ reduction of the bistatic distances that can be
supported at fixed $(P_{\mathrm{d}},P_{\mathrm{fa}})$. Moreover, as stated in
our response to Reviewer~2, Comment~1, the reported loss is an upper bound,
since the known AN echo is discarded. Given the length limit of a
correspondence, we report these values in the text of Section~IV-B rather
than in an additional figure.
\end{response}
